                Future forms <br> (OVERVIEWED)
                present simple
                used to talk about a fixed future event, on a timetable, a schedule or a programme
                present continuous
                used to talk about a future arrangement ( you often mention a time, a date, or a place)
                They're giving prizes to the winners of the cookey competition tomorrow in the Town hall
                future simple
                its usage
                used to talk about a spontaneous decision made at the moment of speaking- you react to circumstances
                Wait for me. I'll help you peel the carrots
                used in questions with I and we, for offers and suggestions, you use shall (not will)
                these bags look heavy, shall i carry them for you?
                used to talk about the future prediction - based on your opinion, prediction or experience
                I can lend you this cookery book but i'll probably need it next week
                be going to + infinitive
                its usage
                used to talk about a future intention (something you have already decided to do)
                - What are you going to prepare for dinner
                - used to talk about a future prediction (based on what you can see or what you know)
                - this cake looks really sweet. Sue isn't going to have any of it

                Question tags
                short questions added at the end of the sentence for comfirmation or to ask a question
                they are formed with an auxiliary verb (be, have, do) or modal verbs (can, will, should) + pronoun (she)
                after a positive statement, you use a negative tag
                You're going to use stale bread, aren't you?
                after a negative statement, you use a positive tag
                You haven't eaten unripe bananas, have you?
                Special cases
                in sentence with i'm, the question tag is aren't I (i am a good cook, aren't i)
                in imperatives, the question tag is will you (Don't order too much food, will you?
                In sentences with let's the question tag is shall we? (let's have some pizza, shall we?)
                in sentece with the pronoun that used as a subject, we use the pronoun it in a question tag (that's pumpkin soup, isn't it?)
                Question tags for confirmation
                they have a failing intonation
                You know how to cook scrambled eggs, don't you? (We are almost certain the person knows how to do it)
                question tags to ask a question
                they have a rising intonation
                your sister is a vegan, isn't she? (We aren't certian the person is vegan)
                You form question tags with an auxiliary verb (be, have, dc) or modal verb (e.g. can, will, should) + pronoun (e.g. I, you, shc). After a positive statement, you use a negative tag, after a negative statement - a positive tag.
Positive statement + negativo tag
You're going to use stale bread, aren't you?
Negative statement + positive tag
You haven't eaten unripe bananas, have you?
Special cases
In sentences with I'm, the question tag is aren't I?. I'm a good cook, aren't I?
In imperatives, the question tag is will you?: Grate some Parmesan cheese, will you?
Don't order too much food, will you?
In sentences with let's, the question tag is shall we?: Let's have a quick snack, shall we?
In sentences with the pronoun that used as a subject, we use the pronoun it in a question tag:
That's pumpkin soup, isn't it?

                Indirect questions <br> (OVERVIEWED)
                used to be more polite
                the word order is the same as in affirmative statements
                Could you tell me what the dates and the times of the next course are
                i would also like to know whether i need to bring anything else
                you use if/whether for yes/no questions (you don't use do/does/did

                Indirect questions
                You can use indirect questions to be more polite:
                Direct: (1)  
                Indirect: Could you tell me what the dates and the times of the next course are? 
                Direct: (2)  
                Indirect: I would also like to know whether I need to bring anything else.
                Note: The word order in indirect questions is the same as in affirmative statements. 
                You use if/whether for yes/no questions. You don’t use do, does or did.

<p>
                <!---->
                A formal email asking for information and clarification <br> (OVERVIEWED)
                Start the email politely
                Dear Sir or Dear Madam
                Dear Sir/Madam (if gender is unknown)
                Dear Mr, Dear Mrs or Dear Miss (to a young woman) + surname
                Dear Ms + surname (to an unmarried woman, or if you are not sure)
                Don't use 
                abbreviations: not Thanks, Thank you
                informal phrases: not lots of, several; a number of
                no I can't wait for, but 
                contractions: not I'm, I am
                in the first paragraph, refer to the letter/email/advert you are responding to and say why you are writing
                Thank you for your letter/email informing me that.../regarding ...
                If something is unclear, ask for clarification
                Could you confirm/explain when/where/what/how/whether/if ...?
                In the final paragraph, mention that you would like a reply.
                I look foward to receiving your reply soon.
                Close the email politely
                yours sincerely (if you know the name of the person you are writting to)
                yours faithfully (if you started the letter with dear sir/madam)
